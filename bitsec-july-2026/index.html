01

Executive Summary

BitSec is Subnet 60 on Bittensor. In plain English, it is a marketplace that pays independent engineers from anywhere in the world to build artificial intelligence that hunts for security flaws in software. It began with the most unforgiving corner of that problem: the smart contracts that hold billions of pounds of Crypto assets. From there, it intends to widen out into ordinary business software.

The investment case for BitSec rests on a single, uncomfortable observation. Software is now being written faster than any human team can review it. AI coding tools have made a competent engineer several times more productive, and a large share of the code they ship was drafted by a machine and never read line by line by a person. The problem is that AI is not only enhancing how code is written. It is also, unfortunately, turbocharging the hacks. Auditing, the discipline of checking that code for exploitable mistakes, has not kept pace. It is still slow, still expensive, and still done largely by hand. The gap between how fast code is created and how fast it is checked is widening every month, and every unchecked line is a place a major loss can hide.

BitSec's answer is to make security a competition. It hands the same problem to hundreds of rival teams, scores their AI agents ruthlessly on whether they find the planted flaws, pays the best performers, and publishes the winning approach so the next team can build on it. Over time the network as a whole becomes a better auditor than any single team could be, and the founder's ambition is for it to eventually match or beat the best closed models on the market at this one narrow task.

What makes BitSec worth Dragonfly's attention is that it is perfectly placed to earn fast-growing revenues by tackling a risk that the widespread adoption of AI is itself creating. The subscription business is early and small, but it is genuine: paying clients have completed the trial phase, the pitch converts well, and the target for the year is a modest book of company subscriptions. If that book grows, BitSec becomes a network with an external revenue engine, which is precisely the kind of transition to real-world use and sizeable revenues that we look for. Against these attractive growth prospects, the risks at this very early stage are equally real, and we set them out plainly below.

02

The Huge Problem That Won't Go Away

In plain numbers: the world spends more than two hundred billion dollars a year on cybersecurity, and the results are still structurally poor. Exploits keep happening, and the losses keep rising. The problem is not a shortage of effort or money. It is that the defence does not scale the way the threat does.

Two things have changed at once. First, code is being produced at a pace that would have been unimaginable a few years ago, because AI now writes or assists with a large fraction of it. Second, that machine-written code is, on the available evidence, less secure than code written carefully by an experienced human. Roughly half of AI-generated code is estimated to contain vulnerabilities. So the volume of code is exploding, and the average quality per line is falling, at the same time.

The traditional response, a human security audit, was already the bottleneck before any of this. A serious audit of a financial codebase can cost anywhere from tens of thousands to millions of pounds and can take weeks or months. By the time it is finished, the software has usually moved on, because development teams ship new features constantly, and the new features were not in the audit. The result is a permanent security debt that a human-only model can never repay. If the only defence against machine-written code is a human reading it afterwards, the defence has already lost.

This matters most acutely in Crypto, where a single missed flaw is not an inconvenience but a catastrophe. When a smart contract holds user funds, one exploitable line can drain the entire protocol in minutes, and the loss is usually irreversible. The industry provides a steady stream of expensive lessons. A rounding error in one protocol's contract logic reportedly enabled a loss of over one hundred million dollars; an integer-overflow flaw in a decentralised exchange's liquidity maths was tied to a loss in the region of two hundred million; and a counterfeiting vulnerability sat undiscovered in a major privacy coin for years before an audit surfaced it. In each case the flaw was a needle in a haystack, and in each case one needle was enough.

The core idea, in one line

We used AI to build the world at machine speed. We now need AI to check it at machine speed, because humans alone never can.

That is the space BitSec is built for. It is not proposing a better one-off audit. It is proposing continuous, automated checking that re-runs every time the code changes, so that security keeps pace with development instead of trailing it by months. The market it is chasing, at least to begin with, is the high-stakes subset of that two-hundred-billion-dollar problem where the buyers already understand that a missed flaw costs far more than the fee.

03

How BitSec Works

BitSec is Subnet 60, one of the 128 specialist AI subnets that currently make up the Bittensor network, and its focus is finding software security flaws. Each of Bittensor's subnets turns a specific hard problem into a paid contest. They do not employ people. Instead, each subnet defines a task, invites anyone in the world to compete at it, scores every entry against an objective measure, and pays out its native token to whoever performs best.

The competitors

Two roles do the work. The competitors, known as miners, each build an AI security agent, in practice a small program that decides which files to examine and what kinds of flaw to look for, and runs inside a sealed sandbox so it cannot do anything other than analyse code. The referees, known as validators, put those agents to the test. They take real and synthetic codebases whose flaws are already known, run each competing agent against them, and record what each agent found and what it missed.

The test, and why it is hard

The scoring is deliberately brutal, and this is the part that gives the mechanism its integrity. It is binary and it is pass-or-fail. If a codebase contains two high-severity flaws, an agent has to find both, and it has to do so reliably, on at least two of three separate runs, to score at all. Anything less scores nothing. This mirrors how real security actually behaves: getting most of the flaws is not a good grade, because the one you missed is the one that drains the protocol. To pass, an agent effectively has to be perfect across a batch of fifty to seventy separate codebases.

The difficulty is not theoretical. When the team tested an early version of the benchmark against a baseline agent built on a leading frontier model, that agent found every high-severity flaw in only three of thirty-one projects. The bar is set where real audits are set, and it is raised as the field improves, which is what stops agents from simply memorising the answers.

The flywheel

The feature that makes the whole thing compound is that the winning agents' code is made open source. Every competitor can see the current best approach and try to beat it. This is the same open, communal design that a sister subnet used to reach the front rank on general coding benchmarks, and there is no obvious reason the same dynamic cannot work for auditing. Better agents earn more; earning more attracts stronger builders; stronger builders produce better agents. BitSec's own framing of the loop is that performance builds trust, trust drives usage, and usage drives revenue and better data, which in turn produces better agents.

For a paying customer the experience is meant to be simple. Point BitSec's scanner at a code repository and, minutes to hours later, receive an actionable report of what is wrong and how severe it is, rather than waiting weeks for a human firm. One important detail for the sceptical reader: the miners never touch a client's private data. BitSec takes the best agents the contest produces, runs them itself on its own infrastructure against the client's code, and it is BitSec, not the anonymous miners, that handles anything confidential.

04

Technology and the Performance Claim

BitSec's second version went live in December 2025 and marked a real architectural step up. The first version proved a narrow point, that AI could find genuine flaws in real code, including in another live network. The second version replaced a single detection model with a full agent-based system: agents that do not merely scan code but chain together workflows, static analysis and fine-tuned models to hunt across different kinds of software. The founder's insight is that the underlying model is only one ingredient. The surrounding machinery, how the agent manages memory and context, how it uses sub-agents and tools, how it is prompted, adds large multipliers on top of a good model. BitSec describes itself as a harness that puts a capable model on stilts.

There is also a meaningful separation between the contest and the product. In the contest, agents are constrained by tight time and cost limits, because the mechanism has to score hundreds of entries efficiently. In production, BitSec removes those limits. It runs a full stack of the best agents, spends hundreds of millions of tokens, and lets an audit take days, deliberately exhausting the edge cases in a way the competition never could. Detection is also only the first step; the fuller service extends to verifying each flaw, producing a proof of concept, and delivering fixes in a form developers can act on.

The Fable 5 comparison, handled honestly

BitSec's most eye-catching marketing claim is a head-to-head against one of the most capable closed frontier models available, Anthropic's Fable 5. By the firm's own account, on a snapshot of one paying client's codebase, Fable 5 surfaced around sixty issues with a small number of high-severity findings, while BitSec's stack surfaced more than one hundred and sixty in total, including several criticals and a number of high-severity flaws Fable 5 missed entirely, and caught everything Fable 5 caught. The founder attributes this to the incentive mechanism: because the contest points miners specifically at the flaws that current tools cannot find, the network keeps extending the frontier of what is detectable, whereas a general-purpose model is not tuned for this one task.

What we cannot substantiate

This comparison is BitSec's own, and the supporting evidence has not been published. The founder states it is client-confidential and that he is still seeking a trusted third party to verify it. In an abundance of caution, we therefore treat the specific numbers as an unverified vendor claim.

The durable signal, for our purposes, is not the Fable 5 comparison, though our research has documented other subnets matching or beating incumbent AI giants. It is the published behaviour of the mechanism itself: a genuinely hard, refreshed, pass-or-fail benchmark on which a strong baseline agent fails most projects, an open-source flywheel with a working precedent on another subnet, and a growing set of real demonstrations, including surfacing the integer-overflow flaw behind a large exchange exploit in under ten minutes and finding access-control and mint-limit errors in live smart contracts. Those are the things we can point to. The claim to have beaten Fable 5 is the kind of assertion that, if ever independently verified, would provide an exceptionally strong price catalyst.

05

Revenues and Commercialisation

This is the section that decides whether BitSec is investable rather than merely clever, so we spend the most time here. Most Bittensor subnets are pure technology-idea stories: the network prints its token, distributes it to participants, and the token's value rests on the hope of future usefulness and product-market fit. BitSec seems primed to break out of this earliest-stage group by earning money from customers, and by the middle of 2026 it had moved from proving the technology to what it calls Phase 1, the push for revenue.

What is actually being sold

The product is a subscription, priced between one thousand and five thousand pounds a month, for continuous scanning of a client's code that re-runs as the code changes. The sales motion is unusually concrete. BitSec looks at a prospect's public code and bug-bounty history, finds real flaws, and presents a side-by-side report at the first meeting, absorbing the upfront cost of that first scan itself. The pitch then writes itself: here is a critical flaw in your own code, a traditional bug-bounty payout on it might cost fifty to a hundred thousand pounds, so instead pay a monthly fee, because your codebase never stops changing and neither should the checking. On the firm's own numbers, that pitch converts qualified prospects at around seventy per cent, which if it holds is a strong signal that the service meets a real and felt need at an attractive price point.

Where the money is expected to come from

Three streams are envisaged. The subscription business is the core. Alongside it sit bug bounties, where the network's agents are positioned to win or facilitate payouts, and audit contests. The commercial sweet spot has settled not on lone developers running one-off scans, nor on the largest enterprises with their long procurement cycles, but on nimble, earlier-stage protocols and teams that are shipping features quickly, cannot justify repeated large audits, and value an always-on service they can afford.

The target, and the honest caveat

The stated goal for the year is a book of around fifty company subscriptions, with the possibility of a first enterprise deal, translating to somewhere between fifty and two hundred and fifty thousand pounds of monthly recurring revenue. That range is wide because it depends entirely on execution. The technology, by the team's account, could already serve a thousand clients tomorrow; the constraint is human, not technical. The operating team is two people, and both a dedicated operations hire and a second developer are described as needed to scale the commercial effort. So the revenue thesis is not a question of whether the product works. It is a question of whether a very small team can build a sales and delivery organisation quickly enough. That is the single most important variable in the entire case, and it is unproven.

If the book is built, the significance goes beyond the revenue itself. It would de-risk the emissions-only narrative that hangs over almost every subnet, and it would deepen the moat, because each real audit feeds the data flywheel that makes the agents better. Real customers and better agents reinforce one another. That is the outcome we are underwriting, with clear eyes about the probability.

06

Tokenomics and Value Accrual

The SN60 token is the instrument through which value in this network is expressed. Mechanically, it is the currency in which miners and validators are rewarded for participation, and it is expected to underpin access to the paid scanning services. Its value accrues along two routes that, importantly, can operate together.

The first route is the network's own emissions. Bittensor issues a fixed daily quantity of its base token across all subnets, and each subnet's share is set by its relative performance, stake and price. BitSec's share is currently modest, in the region of a third of a per cent of network emissions, which is meaningful but mid-tier and leaves clear room to grow if performance improves. This provides a baseline: a floor of value that flows to the subnet and its stakers regardless of external sales, and that rises as the subnet climbs the rankings.

The second route is the external revenue described above, and this is where the design becomes interesting. As subscription income grows, the intended mechanism for returning value to token holders is buybacks, using revenue to purchase SN60 in the market. The team has framed this in terms of alignment: the reward that keeps miners pushing the frontier is funded by the commercial success of the product they enable, so the people improving the technology, the customers paying for it, and the token itself are pulled in the same direction. If emissions are the floor, revenue-funded buybacks are the torque.

Two structural points support conviction and one deserves scrutiny. Supporting it: a high share of the token supply, roughly three-quarters, is staked, which signals holders' commitment rather than intent to sell, and the team holds a large locked position of around six hundred and forty thousand SN60, tying their own upside directly to the token and to revenue growth. Deserving scrutiny: the reward mechanism is winner-takes-all, so each incremental improvement pays a large bounty to the single miner who achieves it. That concentrates rewards and sharpens competition in the early phase, which is useful, but it is a design choice worth watching over time for its effect on the breadth of participation the flywheel depends on.

07

Traction, Team and Ecosystem

The most persuasive evidence for the technology is that it keeps working. On the firm's account, almost anything the system is pointed at yields at least one high-severity flaw and often a critical one, which is encouraging for BitSec and sobering about the state of deployed code generally. Within its home ecosystem, BitSec has been scanning other Bittensor subnets and protocols and publishing audit reports openly on GitHub, and it has run collaborations across the ecosystem, including a smart-contract audit of another subnet's liquidity-vault contracts and work alongside several other projects. The network is fully subscribed, with all of its participant slots filled, which points to a healthy level of competitive interest among builders.

The team is small and technically credible. The founder is a long-time software engineer turned smart-contract developer, who helped scale an earlier technology business and came to this problem the hard way, after a contract he worked on was itself exploited for a six-figure loss and he later found the same flaw using a general AI model for a fraction of the cost. That experience, that AI could find the very exploit that had cost his team dearly, and that this was the least capable AI would ever be, is the origin of the whole venture. He is joined by a senior developer with a data-science and full-stack background and a marketing lead with a Crypto research background. It is a two-person operating core with a clear need to hire, and the founder is candid about that being the binding constraint.

Roadmap, next few months

A brand and website redesign aimed at readers who do not yet know Bittensor; heavier and more regular communication of commercial progress, which the team admits it has under-reported relative to its technical progress; continued expansion of the client book toward the year-end target; and, over a longer horizon, extension from Crypto into ordinary Web2 software, where the addressable market is far larger.

One honest observation on the presentation of all this. BitSec's story is often told, in podcasts and on social media, in language considerably more sensational than the underlying facts support, with talk of defeating hyper-intelligent models and the like. The measured version, an unusually hard benchmark, a working open-source flywheel, real flaws found in real code, and an early but genuine attempt at revenue, is strong enough to stand on its own, and is the only version an institutional reader should weigh.

08

Risks and Early-Stage Reality

We hold ourselves to setting out the reasons this could disappoint as carefully as the reasons it could work. This is an early-stage protocol with a small team, and the risks are correspondingly concrete.

Execution is the dominant risk. The revenue thesis depends on a two-person team building a sales and delivery function fast enough to convert a pipeline into a real subscription book. Hiring is acknowledged as the constraint. If the team cannot scale operations, the technology's capability will not matter, and the year-end target will slip. Everything else is secondary to this.

The flagship benchmark claim is unverified. The comparison against a frontier lab, the single most-repeated selling point, has not been independently confirmed and the evidence remains client-confidential. If it were ever disproved or quietly dropped, it would damage the reputation the whole commercial flywheel is meant to build.

Competition is real and comes from three directions. Other security-oriented subnets exist and could contest the same ground; the general-purpose closed AI tools keep improving and may narrow BitSec's edge; and incumbent human audit firms hold the existing relationships and budgets. BitSec's defence is the combination of an incentivised, decentralised swarm and its open-source data flywheel, but that defence has to be proven in the market, not just in theory.

The token is small, illiquid and volatile. A market capitalisation of a few million pounds and thin trading mean the price can move sharply on modest flows, and the token already trades well below its all-time high. It carries the full beta of the wider Crypto market, is exposed to dilution as supply expands toward its maximum, and its emission share can shift with the network's own evolving rules and periodic issuance reductions. Detection technology itself is imperfect, with false positives and negatives an ongoing engineering challenge that the team addresses through continual improvement. And as a provider of security services, the venture could in time attract regulatory attention, although its open and decentralised character helps.

The honest summary

The current valuation reflects the lack of an established revenue book. The technology looks strong and the mechanism is elegant, but the commercial proof is thin and rests on a very small team. This is a high-risk, high-reward position whose central question is executional, and it should be sized as such.

09

How BitSec Fits Dragonfly's Strategy

Dragonfly is a London-based specialist in liquid Crypto investing, running a long-only book with no leverage and no synthetic exposure, concentrated on altcoins, a sizeable portion of which is in Decentralised AI. We are not traders. We look for productive networks with a plausible path to real revenue, and we are content to own them early, at small size, and to hold for the enormous compound growth that the successful ones can deliver.

BitSec sits naturally inside that mandate. It is a Decentralised AI network in the truest sense, an open, incentivised market that coordinates independent intelligence toward a task no single participant could solve as well alone. More than that, it is a picks-and-shovels position rather than a bet on any one application. Security is infrastructure for the entire era of machine-written software; BitSec's fortunes are tied to the volume of code the world produces and the losses it wishes to avoid, not to whether any particular protocol succeeds. That is exactly the kind of durable, upstream exposure we prefer.

It also has the feature we most want and most rarely find at this stage: a serious attempt to earn money from customers. We size a position like this small, and the thesis is bought on the problem and the mechanism; it is confirmed, or not, by the customer count.

We are comfortable that the market has not yet caught up to this. The story is buried, told in the wrong register, and weighed down by claims that are stronger than the evidence, which is precisely the sort of situation in which a patient, fundamentals-led buyer can build a considered position before the picture clarifies. As long-term investors, it is immaterial - perhaps even advantageous - that this is not yet the consensus view.
