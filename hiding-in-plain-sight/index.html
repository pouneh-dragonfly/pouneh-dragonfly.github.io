Hiding in Plain Sight

The tech boom nobody is pricing

While the world argues about what AI will one day earn, a small corner of Crypto is already earning it, and paying it out. Crypto protocols have crossed from business plans to cash-generating businesses, by solving real problems for paying customers. Almost nobody is looking.

Written by Pouneh Bligaard · 17 November 2026
A word on AI before we begin

We are believers in AI. That is exactly why we look elsewhere.

Let us be clear from the start: we love AI. We use it every day, we build with it, and we fully subscribe to the view that it is the most important technology of our lifetimes. Its leaders are extraordinary businesses. Anthropic, the maker of Claude, grew its annualised revenue from about $47 billion in May to around $81 billion by the end of October, and is pricing its IPO this week at up to around $1.4 trillion. OpenAI's annualised revenue has reached about $50 billion. No technology in history has generated revenue this quickly.

But for an investor, the question is not whether AI is brilliant. It is whether today's price already assumes it, and who will end up keeping the profits. Here the question marks are large. The leading labs are valued at around 17 to 21 times revenue. OpenAI expects to burn roughly $278 billion of cash before the end of the decade. Open-weight models now do most of the work on the main AI marketplaces at a fraction of the price, putting pressure on what the labs can charge. None of them yet returns anything to its owners, and the only ways in are private funding rounds or an IPO priced for perfection.

Now consider Venice. It is a private AI service with more than four million users, profitable since the first quarter of 2026, whose revenue grew roughly tenfold this year to well over $100 million. It trades at around 6 to 10 times revenue, uses its subscription income to buy back and destroy its token, and anyone can buy it, any day, in seconds. It is a Crypto protocol, and most professional investors have never heard of it.

Having your cake and eating it

Investors normally have to choose. Early-stage technology returns come from venture capital: years of lock-up, large minimum tickets and an exit you do not control. Liquidity comes from public markets, where the companies are mature and the explosive growth is usually behind them. And only the most mature companies generate enough spare cash to pay their owners back. Growth, liquidity, income: pick one, perhaps two.

The best Crypto protocols break that rule. Their tokens give holders an economic stake in the network, trade around the clock and can be bought by anyone, in any amount, in seconds. And because a blockchain lets a small team serve customers worldwide from day one, at almost no extra cost per user, these businesses throw off surplus cash unusually early. They can start paying their holders while revenue is still growing fast. Venture-style growth, public-market liquidity and a payout normally reserved for mature companies, in a single asset.

That rare combination is why we are so excited about this sector, and why we do not think it will stay overlooked for long.

This report is about why that gap exists, why we think it is temporary, and why Venice is not alone. Across Crypto, a growing group of protocols has quietly become what the dot-com companies only promised to be: fast-growing technology businesses with real customers, real revenue and, increasingly, a mechanism that hands that revenue back to their owners.

01 · Traction

The Business Plan Era Is Over

For most of its history, Crypto asked investors to buy a promise. The 2021 boom looked a lot like 1999: prices ran on hope, a token was a ticket to a future that had not been built, and very few of the networks being bought were actually used for anything other than trading each other.

That has changed, and the change shows up where every equity analyst looks first: the revenue line. In 2021, around 125 protocols earned any fees at all. By 2025 that number was 1,124, and 1kx counted 1,737 active fee-earning protocols in October 2026. By late 2025, 71 protocols had reached $100 million in annual recurring revenue, 32 of them within a single year.

Just as telling is who earns it. In the early days, nearly all fees went to the blockchains themselves, the digital equivalent of the telecoms companies laying cable. Today the money is made by the applications built on top: trading venues, lending markets, AI services, collectibles marketplaces. Applications' share of sector fees has risen from about a quarter to well over half, exactly as internet value migrated from the fibre-optic builders to Amazon and Google.

02 · Why customers pay

Paid for Solving Real Problems

The revenue is not an accident of speculation. People pay these protocols for the same reason they pay any good business: they fix something that is slow, expensive, unfair or simply impossible in the existing system. The best examples look less like "Crypto projects" and more like early-stage technology companies that happen to settle on a blockchain.

Venice: the private library

4m+users, profitable

The problem: your AI knows too much about you

Every question typed into a mainstream chatbot can be stored, reviewed and, in some cases, handed over to a court. For lawyers, doctors, executives and anyone with something to protect, that is a reason not to use AI at all. Venice runs leading open and closed models on a private architecture: prompts are encrypted on your device, routed through a proxy and not stored on its servers. Think of the difference between a Swiss private bank and a high-street branch. People pay for discretion.

~10xrevenue growth in 2026

The traction: profitable while the giants burn cash

Venice became profitable in the first quarter of 2026, passed $70 million of annualised revenue by July and over $100 million in August. Independent research firm Sacra estimates around $150 million by September, up from about $10 million at the end of 2025. Because it does not train its own frontier models, it avoids the multi-billion-dollar training bills that keep the large labs loss-making.

The payback: every subscription shrinks the supply

A slice of every subscription is used to buy VVV on the open market and destroy it, so more customers means fewer tokens. Its second token, DIEM, gives the holder $1 of AI usage every day, indefinitely: a season ticket for machine intelligence, and a product designed for AI agents as much as for people.

Fixing what holds AI back

The most interesting pattern is how many of these protocols solve problems that AI itself cannot. The AI giants are brilliant at intelligence, but they face four obstacles that their own business models make hard to fix: trust (users do not want their data stored, and cannot verify which model answered them), cost (compute is scarce and concentrated in a handful of data centres), payments (an AI agent has no bank account) and control (one company can switch a capability off). Crypto protocols are being paid to fix each one.

03 · Growth

Faster Than Silicon Valley, Faster Than AI

The AI boom has produced the fastest-growing software companies in history. Lovable and Cursor became famous for how quickly they reached $100 million in revenue. Yet when Frictionless Capital ranked the quickest companies ever to reach that mark, five of the six fastest ran on blockchain rails.

The reason is structural. A protocol launches globally on day one, with no sales force, no bank partners, no app-store approval and no country-by-country licensing. Users arrive with their money already in a wallet. Settlement happens in seconds. The cost of serving the ten-millionth customer is close to zero.

04 · Tokenholder rewards

The Payback Era: Tokens That Pay Their Owners

Revenue alone does not make an investment. Plenty of fast-growing companies never return a penny to shareholders. What makes this moment different is that Crypto protocols have started to behave like mature, cash-generative businesses while still growing as fast as start-ups.

The mechanisms are familiar to any equity investor. Buybacks: Venice uses subscription revenue to buy VVV in the open market, and Hyperliquid routes almost all of its trading fees the same way. Burns, the onchain equivalent of cancelling repurchased shares: Collector Crypt destroyed 5.4% of its circulating supply in a single anniversary burn, and Uniswap's long-awaited fee switch went live across seven chains in August 2026. Revenue sharing, the dividend equivalent: stakers of Sky, Aerodrome and PancakeSwap receive a share of protocol income.

Crypto projects spent a record $638 million on buybacks in the first eight months of 2026 alone, according to Allium Labs data reported by the Financial Times, more than in the whole of the comparable period last year.

05 · Governance

Discipline Is Spreading, Not Fading

A sceptic might say a handful of stars are doing the paying. It is true that two protocols accounted for almost 90% of 2026's buybacks. But the behaviour underneath is broadening. Bitformance tracked every tokenomics change made by Top 100 tokens since 2021 and sorted them into two piles: changes that favour holders (burns, buybacks, fee switches, lower emissions) and changes that hurt them (new emissions, paused mechanisms, dilutive sales).

The incentives explain why. Founders and teams hold large amounts of their own tokens, which fund their hiring, their growth and much of their personal wealth. When the market rewards fee sharing, as it now visibly does, founders behave like the boards of mature companies. As Charlie Munger put it: show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome. Holders also have teeth: in January 2026, governance pressure forced Aave Labs to commit to revenue sharing and buybacks after tokenholders discovered income being diverted to a private company.

07 · Compare and contrast

Same Revolution, Opposite Price Tags

Every investor we meet agrees that AI is the most important technology of our time. They are almost certainly right. That is precisely the problem. As Warren Buffett wrote in 1979, "you pay a very high price in the stock market for a cheery consensus."

Consider the scale of the consensus. Nvidia is worth around $4.4 trillion. OpenAI was valued at $852 billion in March and is reportedly seeking around $1.4 trillion. Anthropic, valued at $965 billion as recently as May, is due to price its IPO this week at a valuation of up to around $1.4 trillion. The four largest hyperscalers are on course to spend roughly $725 to $750 billion on capital expenditure this year, nearly double 2025, much of it on AI data centres.

The revenue is real, and growing faster than any technology in history. OpenAI's annualised revenue reached about $50 billion at the end of September; Anthropic reported roughly $81 billion at the end of October. Nobody should dismiss that. But who keeps the profit is genuinely uncertain. OpenAI has told investors it expects to burn roughly $278 billion of cash between 2026 and 2030. An MIT study found 95% of enterprise AI pilots produced no measurable profit impact. The value may end up with the chip-makers, the cloud providers, the model labs, the application builders, or simply the customers, as happened with airlines and railways: transformative for the world, punishing for many of the investors who financed them.

Two decisions, and only one is easy

Every technology boom asks investors to make two decisions. The first is which technology will change the world. That is the easy one, and the crowd usually gets it right: it was right about railways, right about the internet, and it is almost certainly right about AI.

The second is which part of the stack will keep the profits. That is the hard one, and it is where fortunes are made and lost. In the late 1990s the consensus was that the internet's riches would flow to the telecoms companies laying the cables. They overbuilt, several went bankrupt, and the value went instead to a search engine and an online bookshop. AI faces the same question today. Will the profits go to the frontier labs, the chip-makers, the cloud providers, the applications, or the services built on top? Nobody yet knows, but today's prices assume they do.

There is a twist that makes this even more interesting. While every eye is on the hottest company, the disruption often comes from a cheaper, more efficient way of delivering the same thing: an infrastructure flip. Expensive proprietary servers gave way to Linux on commodity hardware. Long-distance telephone bills gave way to free internet calls. The product stayed the same; who got paid for it changed completely.

It is happening in AI right now. Despite all the talk of a "forever duopoly" of two frontier labs, open-weight models, which anyone can download, run and improve, now do the majority of the work on the leading AI marketplaces. On OpenRouter, their share of tokens processed rose from about 41% in March to around 72% by September. On Vercel's AI Gateway, it rose from 11% in April to 56% in August. And they do it at a fraction of the cost: Vercel's data implies open-weight tokens are around 87% cheaper.

The fairest objection is growth: the AI leaders' revenue is compounding faster than almost anything, and a high multiple on hyper-growth can be cheap. 09 · The biggest growth driver

The Biggest Opportunity of All: Crypto Is the Rails for a Billion Transacting Agents

For three years the stock market has been funding the infrastructure of artificial intelligence: chips, data centres, power stations, frontier models. The obvious question is the one few people ask. What is all of that infrastructure actually for?

Increasingly, the answer is agents. An AI agent is software that does things on your behalf rather than simply answering questions: it books the flight, compares the insurance quotes, buys the dataset, rents an hour of computing power, hires another agent to check its work. Agents are already here. On OpenRouter, one of the largest marketplaces for AI models, agent usage overtook that of humans in February 2026 and has risen roughly fourteenfold in a year. And agents, unlike chatbots, need to pay for things.

The numbers are hard to grasp. The world's working population is roughly 3.3 billion people, and the average person makes around one online transaction a day. The research firm IDC expects more than a billion AI agents to be deployed by 2029, executing over 217 billion actions a day. Even if only a small share of those actions involve a payment, the volume dwarfs anything the human economy has produced.

Why an agent cannot use a bank

Imagine sending a capable new employee to work abroad with no passport, no bank account and no credit history, and asking them to buy a thousand things a day from strangers, each costing a few pennies. That is the position of an AI agent in today's financial system. Four problems make the old rails unworkable.

1

They are strangers

My agent does not know your agent, or who owns it. Payment has to be guaranteed by code rather than by trust or a credit check.

2

They need it now

An agent buys a piece of data and uses it in the same second. Overnight settlement and weekend closures do not work.

3

They cross borders

When an agent in Shanghai pays one in Mumbai, the banks do not talk to each other. A blockchain does, and converts currency in a fraction of a second.

4

They pay in cents

Over 87% of agent payments on x402, the leading protocol, were worth ten cents or less. A card's fixed fee would swallow them whole.

It is telling who has reached the same conclusion. Coinbase and Cloudflare built x402, a standard that lets any website charge an agent instantly in stablecoins, and Stripe has integrated it. In September, BlackRock's digital assets team published "The Machine-Native Economy", describing AI as machine-native intelligence and digital assets as machine-native money, and arguing that agents could become an overlooked source of demand for blockchains and stablecoins. When the world's largest asset manager, a leading online payments company and the biggest US Crypto exchange all point in the same direction, it is worth paying attention.

The early numbers are small but real. By 1 November 2026, x402 had processed 196 million transactions across twelve blockchains. We should be honest about what that is: dollar volumes are still tiny, and analysts at TRM Labs find that much of today's activity is scripted rather than genuinely agentic. This is the dial-up stage. But dial-up is exactly when the best infrastructure investments were made.

The Nasdaq built the roads. Crypto collects the tolls.

Who gets paid? Three groups, all of them in our universe. First, the rails: the networks where agent payments settle, today mostly Base, with Solana second. Second, the services agents buy: private inference from Venice, whose DIEM token is in effect a perpetual compute allowance an agent can hold; verified answers from engy; storage, data and computing power from Bittensor subnets. Third, the venues agents trade on, which are already onchain and open around the clock. In the gold rush, the surest fortunes were made selling picks, shovels and jeans. In the agent economy, the picks and shovels are the most successful Crypto payment rails.

10 · The cheaper way in

If You Only Want AI, Buy It Wholesale

Suppose you are not persuaded by any of this, and believe AI is the only technology worth owning. Even then, there is a cheaper and more specialised way in than paying 17 to 21 times revenue for a frontier lab: decentralised AI protocols.

The best known is Bittensor. The simplest way to understand it is as Uber for artificial intelligence. Before Uber, getting a taxi meant using a fixed, expensive, centrally owned fleet. Uber mobilised the millions of private cars sitting idle and paid their owners per ride. Bittensor does the same for computing power and AI talent: anyone in the world can contribute to a specialised network, called a subnet, and is paid in tokens according to the measured quality of their work. Poor performers are replaced automatically. Each subnet is, in effect, a tiny AI start-up competing in public, every day, on benchmarks anyone can check.

Because the inputs are spare capacity and global talent rather than purpose-built data centres and Silicon Valley salaries, the costs are categorically lower. Our earlier research documented AI coding services priced at around a fifth to a seventh of comparable centralised tools, computer-vision analysis of a full football match for around $10 against tens of thousands of pounds for traditional camera systems, and verified access to open frontier models at up to a sixth of closed-lab prices. These are specialists, not generalists: each subnet does one thing, and is priced like a small company rather than a would-be monopoly.

11 · Why it is overlooked

Why Is Nobody Looking?

Three reasons, each of which we think is temporary.

The reputation lag. Most institutional investors formed their view of Crypto in 2021 or 2022: speculation, collapses, scandals. That view was largely correct then. It is out of date now, in the same way that judging the internet in 2003 by Pets.com would have meant missing Amazon and Google.

The Bitcoin shadow. Bitcoin holds around 60% of the sector's value but under 7% of its fees, under 3% of its daily users and under 0.1% of its transactions. Investors who equate Crypto with Bitcoin are looking at digital gold and missing the businesses entirely.

Scarcity. Only around 20 projects generate $50,000 or more a day in holder revenue. The opportunity set is small, which keeps it off the radar of large allocators. That is exactly what makes it interesting for a specialist.

12 · Competitive advantages

Moats: Real, But Not Permanent

The best protocols enjoy advantages no traditional company can match: global reach from day one, near-zero marginal costs, real-time transparent accounts, and network effects where every new user makes the service better for the rest. Liquidity attracts traders, who attract more liquidity; collectors attract cards, which attract collectors; privacy-conscious users attract the developers who build for them.

But these moats are shallower than those of a mature software company, because much of the code is open source and users can switch at the click of a button. Leadership rotates quickly: in 2025, about a quarter of the top 20 fee earners changed every three months. Uniswap lost share not by failing but by winning more slowly than faster, cheaper rivals. Hyperliquid's quarterly revenue fell about 43% from its Q3 2025 peak to Q2 2026 as competition bit, even while its volumes hit records. This is early-stage technology: the winners win big, but the winners change.

13 · Risks

Risks, Stated Plainly

Revenue is cyclical

Fees track trading activity and prices. 1kx expects total blockchain revenue of about $100bn in 2026, down 28% on 2025, before a recovery in Q3. Revenue fell broadly in line with market values, so valuations did not get cheaper on the way down.

Concentration

Two protocols made almost 90% of 2026's buybacks. At Collector Crypt, 548 wallets supplied 83% of Q3 pack spending. A few big customers leaving would hurt.

Dilution can cancel buybacks

Token unlocks and new issuance can exceed payouts. For several names, scheduled unlocks will still outweigh payouts over the next year, leaving net holder yield negative: Aerodrome −9%, Venice −11%, Lighter −56%. The direction of travel, though, is clearly towards positive as emissions fall and payouts grow.

Thinner rights than equity

Tokens do not carry statutory claims on assets or profits. Some teams, Venice included, raise equity alongside their token, raising the question of who owns what.

The agent economy is early

Agent payment volumes are still tiny in dollar terms, and much current activity is scripted rather than genuinely agentic. The thesis may take years to show up in revenue.

Custody and security

October's $6.4bn Binance hot-wallet theft, attributed to North Korea, showed that centralised exchanges are where the live risk sits. No protocol was broken, but the damage to confidence was real, and it reinforced the perception that Crypto is risky.

Regulation

The Clarity Act did not pass. Agency rules from the SEC and CFTC are filling the gap, but a change of administration or Congress could unwind them.

Most will fail, and prices swing

Of roughly 1,500 investable protocols, only about 20 returned more than $10m to holders last year. Token prices can fall 50% or more in weeks, regardless of fundamentals.

14 · How this fits the Dragonfly approach

Owning the Earners, Not the Index

This report is the logic behind how we run the fund. We screen every liquid protocol with a simple four-box test, the same questions an early-stage technology investor would ask, plus one that only tokens can answer.

1

Growing users

Is the user base growing, ideally exponentially?

2

Network effects

Does each new user make the network more valuable to the rest?

3

Real revenue

Is there real, sustainable, growing revenue?

4

Pays holders

Is there a live or imminent buyback, burn or fee switch?

Venice, Hyperliquid, Pump.fun and Ethena pass all four today. Counter-intuitively, our favourite hunting ground is protocols that pass the first three but not yet the fourth. Collector Crypt is our live example: genuine users, real revenue and growing usage, but no formal revenue share yet. The market has shown repeatedly that tokens re-rate sharply the moment that switch is thrown, as Uniswap and Ethena did this year.

Our research kept pointing to the same conclusion: far more value is now being created by the applications than by the layer-one blockchains they run on. We have rotated the fund accordingly. Holdings of Crypto application tokens now make up 61% of the portfolio, alongside dedicated themes in privacy and decentralised AI. We value tokens on circulating market cap rather than fully diluted value, hold a diversified basket long-only with no leverage, and keep higher-risk positions smaller than average, using liquidity as our main risk lever. The aim is venture-style returns across the basket, without the lock-ups of venture capital. To 30 September, the fund returned 37% in 2026 against 2.1% for the equal-weighted top 100 tokens. Conclusion

Growing Like a Start-Up, Paying Like a Blue Chip

The world's capital has piled into one technology story, AI, at valuations that assume the profits will arrive and that the right investors will keep them. That may prove true. But it is a bet on tomorrow, at a consensus price.

Meanwhile, a few dozen Crypto protocols have quietly become what the dot-com companies only promised to be. They are paid to solve real problems: private AI, liquid collectibles, round-the-clock markets, and the payment rails a billion AI agents will need. They are growing faster than Silicon Valley's best, run by teams a fraction of the size, typically priced at around half the revenue multiple of the AI leaders, and increasingly they hand their revenue back to their owners. They sit in an asset class most professional investors still dismiss.

Most will not survive. The few that do will look, in hindsight, like the Amazon and Google of the 2003 internet: obvious, and cheap. The skill is in finding them, sizing them and staying disciplined while the market makes up its mind.
